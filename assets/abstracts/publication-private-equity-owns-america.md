Private equity investment funds command increasing wealth and reach in the US economy, yet the scope and role of private equity (PE) remain ambiguous. This paper assembles systematic data to describe PE’s shifting strategies and ownership patterns in the US since the 1980s. We theorize four interconnected roles—“corporate raider,” “Main Street invader,” “monopoly builder,” and “shadow owner.” We then document the changing balance among them. We find a shift away from PE’s traditional role as corporate raiders that specialize in restructuring publicly traded firms. Rather, PE investors increasingly agglomerate and rationalize already private “Main Street” companies. PE rarely takes these firms public, instead selling them off to publicly traded incumbents. PE-owned companies also increasingly stay private through longer hold times and sales to other PE funds. PE thus spreads the logic of shareholder value maximization to ever more corners of the economy outside the regulatory and reporting requirements of publicly traded companies.
